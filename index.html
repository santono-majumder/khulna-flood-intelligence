Study Design

Research Methodology

A transparent, step-by-step overview of how this flood susceptibility study was designed, from data collection to model validation.

Step 01
Flood Inventory
165 georeferenced inventory points were compiled: 83 flood-affected locations identified from CPP and BDMC historical records, and 82 confirmed non-flood points through field verification and JRC Global Surface Water analysis. All points are within Khulna District boundary.
Step 02
Conditioning Factors
12 geospatial factors were extracted through Google Earth Engine: Elevation, Slope, Aspect, TWI, SPI, NDVI, LULC, Soil Type, Rainfall, Drainage Density, Distance from Rivers, and Distance from Roads. Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factor (VIF < 5).
Step 03
Train / Test Split
Data was divided using a stratified 80:20 split, yielding 132 training samples and 33 test samples. Stratification ensured proportional representation of flood and non-flood classes in both sets, preventing class imbalance in evaluation.
Step 04
Model Training
Three ensemble machine learning models were trained on the 132 training samples using scikit-learn defaults with minor tuning: Random Forest (n=100 trees), XGBoost (gradient boosting), and LightGBM (gradient boosting, leaf-wise). Models were trained and saved as .pkl files using Google Colab.
Step 05
Validation Approach
5-fold stratified cross-validation was applied to the training set to evaluate model generalisation. The independent 33-sample test set was held out throughout training. AUC-ROC was the primary evaluation metric. CV Mean AUC is reported as the primary measure of model reliability.
Step 06
Explainability (SHAP)
SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values were computed for all three models to explain individual predictions. Factor importance, dependence plots, and interaction heatmaps were generated in Phase 6, revealing the spatial drivers of flood susceptibility at the pixel level.

Methodological Transparency

Model Performance Notes

📋
On the High AUC Values
All three ensemble models demonstrated excellent discriminative performance on the Khulna District flood susceptibility dataset, achieving cross-validation AUC values of 1.000 (Random Forest), 0.9889 ± 0.0222 (XGBoost), and 0.9905 ± 0.0190 (LightGBM). The high predictive performance is likely attributable to the pronounced topographic gradient across the study area, where flood-prone locations are concentrated within low-lying zones (0 to 5 m elevation) while relatively flood-resistant areas occur at higher elevations (7 to 16 m). Such strong elevation-driven separability has been reported in flood susceptibility studies conducted in low-relief deltaic and alluvial environments. Nevertheless, the exceptionally high AUC values should be interpreted with consideration of potential spatial dependence and dataset characteristics, which can influence model performance estimates.
Note prepared as part of this study, May 2026. Consistent with methodological transparency guidelines for ML-based susceptibility mapping.
Research Outcomes

Key Findings

01
Elevation & NDVI Dominate
Elevation and NDVI together account for 64.6% of cross-model average importance (Elevation 35.9%, NDVI 28.7%). This confirms terrain morphology and land surface condition as the primary spatial drivers of flood susceptibility in the Ganges-Brahmaputra Delta.
Source: SHAP analysis, this study. Data: NASA SRTM, Sentinel-2, Google Earth Engine.
02
Sundarbans as Natural Barrier
The Sundarbans mangrove forest in southern Koyra and Dacope correctly shows Very Low susceptibility in the model output. High NDVI and mangrove LULC classification drive these pixels to the lowest risk class, consistent with ecological literature on mangrove flood attenuation.
Source: This study (SHAP). Consistent with: Rahman et al. 2019; IUCN Bangladesh mangrove resilience reports.
03
Coastal Polder Vulnerability
Inhabited polder areas of Koyra and Dacope show Very High susceptibility, consistent with Cyclone Aila (2009) impacts where over 240 km of embankments were breached across Khulna and Satkhira, affecting an estimated 3.9 million people.
Source: BWDB Flood Damage Assessment (2009); UNDP Bangladesh (2009); CPP damage records.
04
Three-Model Consensus
All three models produce consistent spatial risk patterns. CV AUC values of 1.000, 0.9889, and 0.9905 across 5 folds confirm high reliability. The high AUC reflects the strong elevation-based spatial separability typical of the Khulna delta terrain.
Source: This study. 5-fold CV, 165 GPS inventory points, WGS 84.
05
Pixel-Level Accountability
SHAP provides pixel-level, factor-specific explanations: low elevation drives susceptibility upward while dense NDVI vegetation reduces it. This goes beyond black-box predictions, giving planners and policymakers spatially transparent evidence for decision-making.
Source: SHAP framework (Lundberg and Lee, 2017). Applied to all 3 models in this study.
06
North-South Risk Gradient
Southern coastal zones face cyclone surge and tidal flooding; northern zones face monsoon waterlogging from flat terrain and clay-rich soils. These different flood mechanisms require different urban and regional planning responses for effective risk reduction.
Source: Spatial analysis, this study. Flood mechanism classification: BWDB and CPP records.

This project is titled Applying Explainable AI (XAI) Frameworks to Multi-Model Flood Susceptibility Mapping in the Ganges-Brahmaputra Delta. It was completed using QGIS 4.0.2, Python through Google Colab, and Google Earth Engine. Three machine learning models were built and trained on 165 GPS-collected flood inventory points across Khulna District, using 12 geospatial conditioning factors as inputs. The main goal was to go beyond simple black-box predictions and produce a flood risk platform that clearly explains why specific areas are at risk, so that planners and policymakers can make better and more informed decisions for Khulna District, Bangladesh.
